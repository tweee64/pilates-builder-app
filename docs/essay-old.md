## 1. The Seven Pilates Principles and why we use them in a Reformer class

**Flow** is the thread that holds a class together. It is the way one exercise
travels into the next so the hour feels like a single piece of movement rather
than a list of tasks. In practice flow comes from thoughtful links, minimal
spring changes and pre-cueing what is coming, so clients are never left waiting
or guessing. It keeps the heart rate steady, keeps people mentally engaged, and
lets the body move through all planes of movement in an intuitive way.

**Creativity** is the teacher's artistry — how we choose and pair exercises,
use props, layer variations and phrase our language. Two teachers can use the
same exercises and deliver completely different classes. Creativity is what
keeps regular clients curious and what lets us teach authentically rather than
copying someone else's script.

**Alignment** is how the body is organised before, during and after a movement
so joints and surrounding tissue carry the least unnecessary stress. On the
Reformer, where a moving carriage and spring load are involved, good alignment
is the difference between an exercise that strengthens and one that strains. It also
builds proprioception and, over time, more efficient and better-balanced
musculature.

**Breath and control** work as a pair. Steady, focused breathing recruits the
deep core, calms the nervous system, and coordinates movement so it is
deliberate rather than momentum-driven. Changing where we breathe changes the
intention of an exercise and where the challenge sits. Control is not just
performing the exercise; it is performing it with intention and self-awareness,
so clients find their own rhythm instead of rushing.

**Dynamic movement** brings changes of tempo — slow and controlled through to
quick and energetic — and a balance of stability against mobility. This is what
makes a class feel alive and playful, and physiologically it builds strength,
coordination and biomechanical efficiency rather than one quality alone.

**Motivation** is the tone and language that make the room feel encouraging and
inclusive. It is not just cheerleading; it is genuine, well-timed language that
celebrates individual progress so every client, whatever their level, feels
capable and welcome.

**Integration** ties the other six together. A class that is aligned but joyless,
or flowing but unsafe, has missed the point. Integration is delivering all the
principles at once to create a balanced, nurturing class — which is exactly why
we use them: they turn a set of exercises into a coherent, safe and genuinely
transformative experience.

## 2. Anatomy, tactile cueing and balanced musculature

Anatomical knowledge is what makes cueing meaningful. When I can name which
muscles are producing a movement and where they sit on the body, I can explain
*why* we are doing an exercise and *where* it should be felt — which helps
clients engage the right muscles, stop compensating, and avoid strain. It also
empowers them: they leave understanding their own body a little better. The
caveat is translation. Anatomical cues are useless if clients don't know the
vocabulary, so I need to pair them with plain language and inclusive visual cues
that describe a *feeling* rather than a body shape.

Tactile cueing serves the kinaesthetic learners — the clients who simply cannot
find a position from words alone. After group and then individual verbal cues,
a hands-on adjustment is the next step. It can refine alignment, offer support
so someone can safely attempt more, or act as motivation — a hand to lift a leg
towards, or a touch on the shoulder that says "well done." Consent must always
be asked for, discreetly and in advance, and the touch must be purposeful and
clear.

Sequencing for balanced musculature is a safety and longevity issue. Muscles
work in groups with different fibre orientations, so training only one action
creates imbalance. Glutes are the clearest example: hip extension alone leaves
out external and internal rotation, abduction and pelvic stability, so I want
exercises covering all of them across the three gluteal muscles. The same logic
applies across the whole body — the class format we use exists to distribute
time fairly between core, lower body, upper body and mobility, so no single
group dominates. Combined with moving through all planes of motion, this leaves
clients feeling worked but invigorated rather than sore and lopsided.

## 3. The teaching skills and why they matter

**Holding the room** — presence, empathy, self-awareness and warmth — is the
foundation. Clients will only push themselves in a space where they feel safe
and unjudged, and that safety is built by full attention, remembering names and
remembering the class is about them, not me.

**Cueing** is the backbone: set-up, directional, alignment, counter, motivational
and pre-cues each do a different job, and using them in the right order is what
makes an exercise both effective and safe without stalling the flow.

**Alignment cueing**, **observation** and **hands-on adjustments** are the safety
net. Observation is the watchful eye that catches fatigue, notices who needs a
modification and who is ready to layer up, and adapts for special populations in
real time. Adjustments then fix what the eye has found.

**Demonstration** bridges the gap when words fall short — ideally mirrored from
the floor or through a client, so I stay present in the room instead of
disappearing onto a machine.

**Active teaching** — moving through the room, positioning myself where the class
is facing, using mirrors — ensures everyone feels individually seen.

**Motivation and personality** (voice projection, tone, pitch, music and
authenticity) give the class its feel, and **creativity and flow** give it its
shape.

Finally, **always be a student**. These are skills, not traits, and they only
improve through continued education, mentorship, staying informed and keeping
up my own practice. Refining them is what turns a competent instructor into one
whose classes people come back for — and being a student myself keeps me
connected to what my clients actually experience.
