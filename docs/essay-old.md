## 1. The Seven Pilates Principles and why we use them in a Reformer class

**Flow** is what holds a class together. It's the way one exercise travels into
the next so the hour feels like a single piece of movement instead of a list of
tasks. In practice that comes down to thoughtful links between exercises, as few
spring changes as possible, and telling clients what's coming before it arrives
so nobody is left waiting or guessing. It keeps the heart rate steady, keeps
people mentally in the room, and lets the body move through all planes of
movement without anything feeling forced.

**Creativity** is the teacher's artistry. It shows up in how we choose and pair
exercises, how we use props, how we layer variations, and how we phrase things.
Two teachers can run the same exercises and deliver completely different
classes. Creativity is what keeps regular clients curious, and it's what lets me
teach as myself rather than reciting someone else's script.

**Alignment** is how the body is organised before, during and after a movement
so the joints and the tissue around them carry the least unnecessary stress. On
the Reformer, with a moving carriage and spring load in the mix, good alignment
is the difference between an exercise that strengthens and one that strains. It
also builds proprioception, and over time it produces more efficient, better
balanced musculature.

**Breath and control** work as a pair. Steady, focused breathing recruits the
deep core, calms the nervous system and coordinates movement so it's deliberate
rather than driven by momentum. Changing where we breathe changes the intention
of an exercise and shifts where the challenge sits. Control means performing the
exercise with intention and self-awareness, so clients find their own rhythm
instead of rushing through.

**Dynamic movement** brings changes of tempo, from slow and controlled through
to quick and energetic, along with a balance of stability against mobility.
That's what makes a class feel alive and playful, and physiologically it builds
strength, coordination and biomechanical efficiency rather than only one of
those qualities.

**Motivation** is the tone and language that make the room feel encouraging and
inclusive. Done well it's genuine and well-timed, and it celebrates individual
progress so every client, whatever their level, feels capable and welcome.

**Integration** ties the other six together. A class that is beautifully aligned
but joyless has missed the point, and so has one that flows but isn't safe.
Integration means delivering all the principles at once to create a balanced,
nurturing class. That's exactly why we use them. They turn a set of exercises
into something coherent, safe and genuinely transformative.

## 2. Anatomy, tactile cueing and balanced musculature

Anatomical knowledge is what makes cueing meaningful. When I can name which
muscles are producing a movement and where they sit on the body, I can explain
*why* we're doing an exercise and *where* it should be felt. That helps clients
engage the right muscles, stop compensating and avoid strain. It also empowers
them, because they leave understanding their own body a little better. The
caveat is translation. Anatomical cues are useless if clients don't know the
vocabulary, so I need to pair them with plain language and inclusive visual cues
that describe a *feeling* rather than a body shape.

Tactile cueing serves the kinaesthetic learners, the clients who simply cannot
find a position from words alone. After a group cue and then an individual
verbal cue, a hands-on adjustment is the next step. It can refine alignment,
offer support so someone can safely attempt more, or work as motivation: a hand
to lift a leg towards, or a touch on the shoulder that says "well done." Consent
has to be asked for every time, discreetly and in advance, and the touch itself
needs to be purposeful and clear.

Sequencing for balanced musculature is a safety and longevity issue. Muscles
work in groups with different fibre orientations, so training only one action
creates imbalance. Glutes are the clearest example. Hip extension on its own
leaves out external and internal rotation, abduction and pelvic stability, so I
want exercises covering all of those across the three gluteal muscles. The same
logic applies across the whole body, which is why the class format we use exists
in the first place: it distributes time fairly between core, lower body, upper
body and mobility so no single group dominates. Combined with moving through all
planes of motion, that leaves clients feeling worked but invigorated instead of
sore and lopsided.

## 3. The teaching skills and why they matter

**Holding the room** comes first. Presence, empathy, self-awareness and warmth
are the foundation, because clients will only push themselves somewhere they
feel safe and unjudged. That safety is built out of small things: giving people
your full attention, remembering names, and remembering the class is about them
and not about me.

**Cueing** is the backbone. Set-up, directional, alignment, counter, motivational
and pre-cues each do a different job, and using them in the right order is what
makes an exercise effective and safe without stalling the flow.

**Alignment cueing**, **observation** and **hands-on adjustments** are the safety
net. Observation is the watchful eye that catches fatigue, notices who needs a
modification and who is ready to layer up, and adapts for special populations in
real time. Adjustments then fix what the eye has found.

**Demonstration** bridges the gap when words fall short. Ideally it's mirrored
from the floor or shown through a client, so I stay present in the room instead
of disappearing onto a machine.

**Active teaching** means moving through the room, positioning myself where the
class is facing, and using the mirrors, so everyone feels individually seen.

**Motivation and personality**, meaning voice projection, tone, pitch, music and
authenticity, give the class its feel, while **creativity and flow** give it its
shape.

Finally, **always be a student**. These are skills rather than traits, and they
only improve through continued education, mentorship, staying informed and
keeping up my own practice. Refining them is what turns a competent instructor
into one whose classes people come back for, and staying a student myself keeps
me connected to what my clients are actually experiencing.
