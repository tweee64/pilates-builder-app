## 1. The seven Pilates principles

Flow is probably the one I understood last and use most now. For a while I thought it just meant "keep moving," but it's really about what happens in the gaps between exercises. Which spring I've asked for, whether I've already told the room what's coming, how the carriage is left sitting when someone stands up off it. Get those wrong and a class turns into a series of stops, which people feel even if they can't name why. Get them right and an hour goes by and nobody checked the clock once.

Creativity is mine to bring, not the manual's. Two teachers with the same exercise list will run two completely different classes, because the choices in between are personal: what gets paired with what, which prop comes out, how a cue gets phrased so it lands. I've noticed regular clients can tell within a minute if I'm running someone else's sequence rather than my own, and they're right to notice.

Alignment is how the body is set up before, during and after a movement so the effort goes where it's supposed to go and not into a joint that's just along for the ride. On a Reformer this matters more than on the mat, because there's a moving carriage and a spring pulling back, and a shoulder that's slightly off in feet-in-straps will get punished for it in a way gravity alone never would. Over a few weeks of consistent alignment cues, people stop needing the cue at all.

Breath and control go together and I'd struggle to separate them if asked to. Breath recruits the deep core and keeps a nervous system from bracing, and control is the discipline of doing the exercise on purpose rather than letting momentum do it for you. Where you place the exhale changes the whole feel of a movement (exhale on the press-out of a hundred is a different exercise to exhale on the return), which is a small thing to notice and a big thing to teach.

Dynamic movement is the range between very slow and quite fast, between stable and mobile, and it's what keeps a class from turning into an endurance test. Without it, everything starts to feel the same regardless of how hard people are working.

Motivation is tone, not cheerleading. Clients can hear the difference between "great job!" said at everyone and a specific, true thing said to one person about what they just did. The second kind is the one that gets remembered on the drive home.

Integration is all of the above landing in the same class at the same time. It's easy to nail one principle and drop the others without noticing, an aligned class can still be joyless, a flowing one can still be unsafe, and integration is the discipline of not letting that happen. That's really the whole argument for using these seven at all: on their own they're just good habits, together they're what makes a class feel like something rather than a checklist someone worked through.

## 2. Anatomy, tactile cueing, and sequencing for balanced muscles

I used to think anatomy knowledge was mostly for sounding credible, and I don't think that anymore. It's what turns a cue from a guess into something a client can actually use. If I know which muscle is meant to be doing the work and roughly where it sits, I can tell someone why we're doing this exercise and where they should feel it, and that's usually enough to stop a client from letting their hip flexors do a glute's job. There's a real payoff for them too: they leave the room understanding their own body slightly better than they walked in.

The catch, and it took me a while to see this, is that anatomical language on its own doesn't help anyone. Saying "engage your transverse abdominis" to a first-timer gets a blank look, not a contraction. So the knowledge has to travel out of my mouth translated, into plain words and a feeling rather than a Latin name. That translation is honestly the harder skill of the two.

Tactile cueing exists for the clients that no amount of clever phrasing will reach, and there are always a few of those in a room. My order is usually a group cue, then something said just to that person, and only then a hand. Touch can correct alignment, or it can support someone into a range they wouldn't try alone, or sometimes it's nothing more than my palm held just above where a leg should reach to. Consent gets asked quietly before the class starts, never mid-movement when it would put someone on the spot, and once given, the touch itself needs to be clear and confident. A vague, hesitant hand does less good than none at all.

Sequencing for balanced musculature is where the anatomy and the class-planning parts of the course actually meet. Muscles don't work as single units, they work as groups pulling in different directions, and building a class around one action over and over makes someone strong in a line and weak everywhere around it. Glutes are the easiest example to picture: a class full of bridges trains hip extension and leaves out abduction, rotation, and the single-leg stability work that actually holds someone up when they're walking. So I want all three gluteal muscles covered somewhere in the hour, not just the one bridges are good at. Zoom out and the same logic is why the class format spreads time across core, lower body, upper body and mobility instead of picking a favourite, and why moving through more than one plane matters. Done properly, people leave worked but not lopsided, tired in a way that feels earned rather than one-sided.

## 3. Teaching skills

Holding the room comes before anything technical, because nobody pushes themselves somewhere they feel watched rather than supported. That's presence and warmth more than any single skill, remembering names, remembering whose hour it actually is.

Cueing is the backbone underneath everything else. Set-up, directional, alignment, counter, motivational, pre-cue, each does a different job, and the order I stack them in is what keeps an exercise safe without ever making it feel like it's stalled to explain itself.

Observation and hands-on adjustment work as a pair, one finding the problem and the other fixing it. Observation is what catches someone fatiguing before they say anything, or the client who's clearly ready to progress but hasn't asked, or the pregnant client whose whole session needs rethinking on the spot. Demonstration fills the gap when a cue just isn't landing, and I try to do it mirrored, from the floor or through a client, rather than disappearing onto a machine at the front of the room, because the moment I do that I've stopped teaching the people in front of me.

Active teaching, walking the room, standing where the class is actually facing, using the mirrors properly, is what makes sure everyone gets looked at at least once, not just the people near the front.

Motivation and personality are what give a class its actual feel, the voice, the tone, whatever music is on, whoever I happen to be on that particular day. Creativity and flow give it its shape. None of this works without the other.

And then there's the part that never finishes: staying a student. These aren't traits you either have or don't, they're skills, and they only sharpen through mentoring, further courses, watching how other teachers solve the same problems, and keeping up a personal practice of my own. That last one might be the most honest reason to keep doing it. It's the only way I actually stay in touch with what it feels like on the other side of my own cues.
